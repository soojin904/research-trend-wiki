## 초록

Social competence is now widely recognised as a central outcome of special education, with implications that extend across the academic, emotional, and occupational trajectories of learners with disabilities. The pedagogical infrastructure through which social competence is cultivated includes a diverse set of practices that address discrete social skills, reciprocal interaction routines, and cooperative play arrangements. This paper synthesises the conceptual and empirical literature on three interlocking strands of the social competence curriculum: the teaching of social skills through explicit instruction and structured practice; the development of turn-taking as a foundational reciprocal competence; and the promotion of cooperative play as the most developmentally rich context within which social skills are consolidated and generalised. The paper traces the theoretical foundations of the social competence construct, examines the methodological traditions that have produced the contemporary evidence base, articulates the pedagogical logic of each of the three strands, and considers the conditions under which integration across the strands is most likely to produce generalised and durable gains. Attention is given to the role of peer models, to the distinctive considerations of autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability, and to the ecological conditions that support or undermine the transfer of social skills from instructional settings to everyday life. The paper is explicitly conceptual; no primary data are presented, and its claims are offered as a scaffold for further enquiry and for the design of pedagogical programmes
