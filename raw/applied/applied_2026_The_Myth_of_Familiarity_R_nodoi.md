## 초록

This article examines how intra-faith marginalization operates within South–South international student mobility, challenging the assumption that shared religion and linguistic proximity facilitate belonging. Drawing on an autoethnographic study of an Indonesian Muslim scholarship student in Malaysian Islamic higher education, the analysis introduces the myth of familiarity as a critical conceptual framing, describing how shared religious or cultural identity may produce more granular hierarchies of legitimacy, thereby contradicting the belief that similarity guarantees inclusion. Using neo-racism and symbolic violence, three narrative vignettes illustrate how hierarchies of piety, nationality, and class are enacted and normalized within a shared Islamic context. The findings show that familiarity does not reduce exclusion but reorganizes it into subtle distinctions of belonging, where acceptance is contingent upon proximity to dominant forms of Muslimness. This study contributes to comparative and international higher education scholarship by showing how sameness itself serves as a mechanism of differentiation in faith-based settings.
