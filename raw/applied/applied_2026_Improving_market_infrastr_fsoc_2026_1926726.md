# Improving market infrastructure for small entrepreneurship in Uzbekistan: a sociological opinion on export potential, employment, and social inclusion

**저자**: Firuza Saidkulova; Dilafiruz Narzieva; Nigina Chorieva; Firuza Achilova; Jasurbek Jonibekov; Mokhichekhra Rajabova
**출처**: Frontiers in Sociology, Vol.11

## 초록

Uzbekistan has moved further and faster on small-business reform than most countries in the post-Soviet region. Between 2017 and 2025 the share of small business in GDP rose from roughly one third to over half, and the number of registered small entities reached 1.21 million by October 2025 (State Statistics Agency, 2025). The country was recognised as one of the five largest global improvers in the 2024 Women, Business and the Law index, with an 11.9-point gain taking its score to 82.5 (World Bank, 2024). The Uzbekistan-2030 strategy targets a doubling of exports and an increase in the number of exporting enterprises from roughly 5,700 to 15,000 (Ministry of Investment and Foreign Trade, 2024).These figures have been read almost exclusively through an economic and engineering lens. The dominant policy frame treats market infrastructure as a matter of roads, customs procedures, payment systems, credit lines and digital platforms. That frame is not wrong. It is incomplete in a specific and consequential way, and the way it is incomplete is sociological. This article makes a conceptual argument rather than reporting new data. Our contribution is not the observation that markets are socially embedded, which has been established since Polanyi (1944) and Granovetter (1985). It is threefold and more specific.First, we argue that the social layer of market infrastructure should be treated as infrastructure in its own right, not as context surrounding the "real" infrastructure. This extends to a post-Soviet setting the argument developed by Elyachar (2010) for Cairo, where the communicative channels produced by women's everyday sociality function as economic infrastructure in the same register as roads and telephone lines, and it connects that argument to Star's (1999) and Larkin's (2013) insistence that infrastructure is relational and becomes visible only through the practices that sustain or fail to sustain it.Second, we specify the mechanism by which social structure acts on formally universal infrastructure. We call it differential access filtering, and we decompose it using the four sources of social capital distinguished by Portes and Sensenbrenner (1993), namely value introjection, bounded solidarity, reciprocity exchanges and enforceable trust. Two of these enable market participation and two constrain it, which gives the framework an internal reason to expect both positive and negative effects rather than asserting one or the other.Third, we propose Uzbekistan as a scope condition for embeddedness theory. Most of the informality and embeddedness literature has examined settings where formal institutions were weak, absent or decaying (Mair and Martí, 2009;Williams and Shahid, 2016). Uzbekistan presents the inverse configuration, in which formal market infrastructure is being built rapidly and from above onto a dense, legally codified communal institution. The theoretical question this raises is whether embeddedness under rapid state-led formalisation produces bridging or closure, and our answer is that it systematically produces both, with the balance determined by which of the four mechanisms a given reform activates.The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framing. Section 3 sets out the fivelayer framework and its social mediators. Section 4 reads the mahalla symmetrically. Section 5 applies the framework differentially to five entrepreneurial positions. Section 6 states explicitly what the available evidence does and does not support. Section 7 discusses feasibility of the policy implications. Section 8 sets out the theoretical contribution, limitations, testable propositions and research agenda.Infrastructure studies in sociology and anthropology have moved decisively away from treating infrastructure as a stock of physical assets. Star (1999) argued that infrastructure is relational rather than substantive, that it is embedded in other structures and practices, and that its distributional properties become visible mainly at the point of breakdown. Larkin (2013) extended this by treating infrastructures as objects that carry political and aesthetic meaning as well as goods, and by showing that the promise attached to an infrastructure is part of what it does. Graham and Marvin (2001) documented the process by which networked infrastructure, far from producing uniform access, splinters into premium and residual circuits. Elyachar (2010) took the argument one step further in a way that is directly relevant here. Studying microfinance and empowerment projects in Cairo, she showed that the communicative channels generated by women's routine sociality constitute a social infrastructure that carries economic traffic as surely as a road does, and that development interventions make this infrastructure visible precisely by attempting to capture or format it. We take this as our starting point. If social ties are infrastructure, then a five-layer account of market infrastructure is not a four-layer account plus context. It is an account of five infrastructures, one of which happens to be made of relationships.Economic sociology supplies the mechanism. Granovetter (1985) established that economic action is embedded in concrete networks of relations. Portes and Sensenbrenner (1993) then did the analytical work that the embeddedness literature most often omits, distinguishing four mechanisms by which social structure shapes economic action and, crucially, identifying their positive and negative consequences together. Value introjection and bounded solidarity generate commitment and mutual support but also generate demands for conformity. Reciprocity exchanges and enforceable trust lower transaction costs but also impose obligations that can drain the successful and restrict the individual autonomy on which enterprise growth depends. Portes and Landolt (1996) and Portes (1998) developed the resulting downsides, namely exclusion of outsiders, excess claims on group members, restrictions on individual freedom and downward levelling norms. We adopt this four-mechanism decomposition as the analytical core of our framework, because it is what allows a symmetrical reading of communal institutions rather than a celebratory one.Two further strands complete the framing. The contextualisation programme in entrepreneurship research insists that where and when entrepreneurship happens is constitutive rather than incidental (Welter, 2011;Welter et al., 2019), and Kloosterman's (2010) mixed embeddedness perspective shows how the resources an entrepreneur can mobilise from social networks interact with the opportunity structure that markets and regulation supply. Meanwhile, the entrepreneurial ecosystem literature has become the dominant policy vocabulary for the phenomena we describe, and its own systematic reviews concede that it remains largely atheoretical and built on advanced-economy assumptions, with resource scarcity, structural gaps and institutional voids distinguishing emergingeconomy ecosystems (Cao and Shi, 2021;Wurth et al., 2022). Our framework is intended in part as a sociological supplement to that literature, replacing its metaphor of an ecosystem of actors with an account of layered infrastructures and the filters that regulate access to them.By market infrastructure we mean the bundle of arrangements that allow a small firm to find customers, set prices, secure finance, manage logistics and enforce rights. We distinguish five layers. The physical layer covers roads, warehouses, bazaars and border posts. The financial layer covers banks, microfinance, payment instruments and credit lines. The digital layer covers e-commerce, platform identifiers, e-government and data systems (Nambisan, 2017;Bonina et al., 2021). The institutional layer covers registration, taxation, standards and dispute resolution. The social layer covers trust networks, reputational systems, professional associations, rotating savings groups and the mahalla.The analytically important claim is not the existence of five layers but the relation between them. Access to any of the first four layers is mediated through the fifth, and the fifth distributes that access unequally. A credit assessment reads a reputation that is produced in a network. A customs procedure is navigated with the help of brokers and peers who have navigated it before. A digital marketplace is adopted first by entrepreneurs whose existing ties already include early users, which is why platform adoption tends to follow pre-existing network structure rather than to disrupt it (Bonina et al., 2021).Where public investment addresses the first four layers without engaging the fifth, returns are eroded by filtering rather than by underprovision. The new road serves the bazaar of the better connected. The new credit line reaches firms with formal collateral. The new platform recruits younger urban men with smartphones and foreign-language skills. This is the splintering dynamic that Graham and Marvin (2001) described, produced here by social rather than commercial sorting.Urban evidence from Uzbekistan supports this reading at the level of the layers themselves. Farmanov et al. (2025), examining smart-city implementation in Bukhara, find that deployment of sensors, platforms and digital governance tools has run ahead of the inclusivity and citizen-engagement components, so that a technically advancing infrastructure is not correspondingly more usable. Dushanova et al. (2025) document the same asymmetry in the city's tourism infrastructure, where heritage sites, transport and wayfinding remain inaccessible to people with mobility impairments despite a national policy commitment to barrier-free provision. Both cases show a filter that is not social in the network sense but produces the same result, namely infrastructure that is formally universal and practically selective. They also support treating market infrastructure as a sociotechnical assembly rather than a technical one, which is the premise of Section 2.In the Uzbek case we identify five social mediators through which the filtering runs, namely mahalla networks, gender norms and the unpaid care burden, the urban-rural divide, the informal economy, and labour migration. These are not causes in a statistical sense and we do not claim to have estimated their effects. They are the channels through which formally universal infrastructure becomes differentially accessible, and Section 6 states precisely how much evidence stands behind each.Figure 1. Which side dominates depends on the mechanism a given reform activates.mahalla solidarity has historically rested on residence rather than kinship or ethnicity, which gave it an unusually broad reach across social groups. Urinboyev and Eraliev (2022) describe the mahalla as a legally plural arena in which competing orders coexist and produce new organisational forms, some of them generative.On the restricting side, the record is at least as substantial and considerably older than our earlier draft acknowledged. Three findings matter for market infrastructure specifically.First, the mahalla's incorporation into public administration has converted an association of residents into an instrument of state administration and, in the assessment of a large body of work, of social control (Urinboyev, 2011;Noori, 2006;Human Rights Watch, 2003). Urinboyev and Eraliev (2022) situate this within broader debates on how non-democratic states neutralise civil-society institutions through administrative and legal incorporation. An entrepreneur's relationship with the mahalla is therefore not a purely horizontal relationship among peers. It carries a vertical dimension, and any policy that routes market infrastructure through the mahalla routes it through that vertical dimension too.Second, mahalla-based discretionary allocation has documented exclusionary effects along gender lines. Kamp (2004) shows that the delegation of welfare targeting to mahalla committees, staffed by untrained neighbours exercising discretion, placed divorced and abandoned women at particular risk of exclusion, and that solidarity among women in a mahalla cannot be assumed. This is bounded solidarity and enforceable trust operating exactly as Portes and Sensenbrenner predicted, producing support for insiders and exclusion at the boundary. If discretionary neighbourhood assessment produces this pattern in welfare, there is no principled reason to expect a different pattern when the same bodies certify cooperatives, endorse borrowers or vouch for exporters.Third, the conformity costs fall unevenly. Restrictions on individual autonomy and downward levelling norms are the two downsides of social capital most directly relevant to enterprise growth (Portes, 1998;Portes and Landolt, 1996). Ethnographic work on women traders in Uzbek bazaars describes exactly this tension, with women negotiating and renegotiating patriarchal norms rather than simply being enabled or simply being blocked by them (Kandiyoti, 2007).The implication for policy is that "recognise the mahalla" is an underspecified instruction. What matters is which mechanism a given reform activates. Instruments that work through reciprocity exchange, such as linking gap groups to formal microfinance, plausibly extend access. Instruments that work through discretionary gatekeeping, such as making mahalla endorsement a condition of registration, credit or certification, plausibly reproduce existing exclusions and add a vertical dependency. We return to this distinction in Section 7.A framework that applies identically to everyone explains nothing about distribution. Three points follow from the table. The binding constraint differs by position, so a single infrastructure package cannot be expected to have uniform effects. The same mechanism that helps one position hinders another, which is why a framework built on Portes and Sensenbrenner's symmetric account is more useful here than one built on social capital as an unambiguous good. And the reforms most likely to backfire are, in several rows, the reforms currently most favoured, namely discretionary community endorsement and platform-first digitalisation.Two of the three policy outcomes named in the title deserve explicit grounding, since the framework is meant to bear on them.Export concentration is documented. In 2024 gold, services and industrial goods together accounted for roughly 70 percent of total exports (Observatory of Economic Complexity, 2025; U.S. Department of Commerce, 2025), and the number of exporting enterprises stood at around 5,700 in mid-2024 against the 2030 target of 15,000 (Ministry of Investment and Foreign Trade, 2024). The shortfall is usually read as a logistics and certification problem. Our framework suggests it is also a trust problem, since enforceable trust built on personal relationship and cash does not travel across borders and infrastructure that supplies no substitute concentrates gains among firms already connected to international buyers. The concentration is a fact; its attribution to social filtering is our proposition.Employment is where the filters compound.
