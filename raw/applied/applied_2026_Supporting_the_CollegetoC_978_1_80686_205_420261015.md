## 초록

Abstract Dominant narratives often portray the bachelor’s degree as a pathway to upward mobility and stable employment. These accounts frequently overlook the intersecting influences of race, class, gender, and first-generation status, obscuring the barriers Black first-generation college graduates face when transitioning from college to career. For instance, Black graduates are disproportionately represented in the lowest earning categories across industries, with 40% struggling to repay student loans. Despite this, few studies document their job search experiences or early workplace challenges. This chapter, grounded in critical race theory, amplifies the college-to-career counternarratives of six Black first-generation graduates who entered the workforce between 2011 and 2017. Through two-hour interviews, participants shared their journeys from initial career aspirations to their ongoing pursuit of “decent work.” Defined by the International Labor Organization, decent work includes fair compensation, healthcare access, adequate time off, and safe, respectful work environments. Findings reveal that due to enduring racism and classism in the US, many Black first-generation graduates find these conditions largely inaccessible. The chapter aims to illuminate the varied paths these graduates take as they navigate predominantly White institutions and professional spaces. It also explores how participants leveraged community cultural wealth—such as familial support, resilience, and social networks—to overcome barriers to equitable educational and workplace support. Their stories underscore the complex, shifting nature of decent work and the critical role of cultural assets in resisting systemic exclusion. Ultimately, this chapter contributes to a deeper understanding of how Black first-generation graduates persist and thrive despite structural inequities.
