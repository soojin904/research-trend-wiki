# Study 2: Prosociality and the effects of social identity distance

**저자**: Luke W. Galen
**출처**: Open MIND
**발행일**: 2026-07-26
**DOI**: https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/qaxjw
**수집 키워드**: social network

## 초록

Previous research has indicated that religiosity is associated with prosociality, such as greater levels of reported agreeableness and empathy. However, there are indications that religiosity also increases parochialism – relatively greater concern for familiar ingroup members and lower concern for those who are socially distant, such as foreigners or outgroup members. Identifying parochial discrepancies as a function of differing identities of the target or referent of prosociality can be difficult because of ambiguity in self-report item referents. Standard self-report measures of traits such as agreeableness, empathy, and altruism typically consist of items that lack specificity regarding the identity of the referent (e.g., respondents’ endorsement of themselves as “Trusting most people”, “Feeling sympathy for others”). Results from a previous study (https://osf.io/9qscm/registrations) indicated that, compared to the nonreligious, highly religious respondents rated themselves as being more prosocial when items referred to familiar ingroup members (“trust in those like me”), but less prosocial than the nonreligious when items referred to unfamiliar outgroup members (“trust in those who are different”). When items lack a specific referent identity, respondents’ inferences about the hypothetical identities of the referents could subjectively vary, with some participants self-evaluating their own prosocial attitudes by thinking of familiar individuals, whereas others could base their responses on attitudes towards unfamiliar or outgroup referents. A consequence of this variability is that the process by which participants generate their inferred reference group will be biased by other characteristics. For example, respondents whose backgrounds consist of a relatively narrow social network may rate themselves as highly prosocial because they have in mind a parochial reference group (i.e., only familiar others), whereas those with more cosmopolitan social backgrounds will use a more diverse reference group (e.g., diverse people living in my city). Therefore, it is necessary to systematically inquire about the reference group used (i.e., “who did you have in mind when you thought of ‘others’?”), cross-referencing this with other participant characteristics, namely religiosity. This could reveal whether variation in respondents’ prosocial expression is actually produced by differences in their inferred reference group, which varies as a function of their religiosity.
