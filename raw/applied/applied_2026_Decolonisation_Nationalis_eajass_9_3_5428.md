## 초록

Decolonisation and the struggle for independence in Kenya involved both men and women. While a lot has been studied and written on the role of women and men in the decolonisation process in the country, very little attention has been paid to how the decolonisation process and politics impacted the construction of masculinities. This article examines the ways in which the politics of decolonisation in Kenya reshaped masculine identities among the Akamba of Machakos County between 1945 and 1963. Drawing on oral histories, focus group discussions and colonial archival sources, it argues that African nationalism, military service during the Second World War, urban migration and wage labour, missionary education, and colonial surveillance produced multiple, overlapping and contested masculinities. The article further argues that these emergent forms of manhood disrupted elder-based authority, transformed domestic gender relations and generated tensions between collaboration, resistance and social mobility. Rather than a linear transition from “traditional” to “modern” masculinity, this period produced hybrid masculine identities negotiated across political, economic and domestic spheres. The article contributes to debates on African nationalism, social change and gender by foregrounding masculinity as a key lens for understanding social transformation during the late colonial period
