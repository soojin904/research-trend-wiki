# Adolescents' perspectives on self-regulation and digital media use

**저자**: Bar Shutzman-Agasi; Naama Gershy Tsahor
**출처**: Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, Vol.4
**발행일**: 2026-07-29
**DOI**: https://doi.org/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1744398
**수집 키워드**: social network

## 초록

Introduction Given the harmful consequences of problematic digital media (DM) use on children's cognitive, emotional, and social development, and the highly addictive nature of the digital environment, children's ability to develop self-regulation in this context is critical. While recent research has begun to explore self-regulation in digital settings, we still know very little about the self-regulatory strategies adolescents employ. This study addressed this pressing gap by investigating the self-regulatory strategies that adolescents use to navigate everyday conflicts related to DM use. Methods One hundred fifty-one adolescents (aged 12–18) were asked to answer three open-ended questions about how they deal with conflicts between their desire to use DM for social and recreational purposes and academic and social demands, as well as how they manage negative emotions arising during DM use. We used a mixed-methods design to analyze the responses, combining qualitative analysis using a constant comparative approach with quantitative analysis examining age-related differences in the response distribution. Results The qualitative analysis revealed five families of self-regulatory strategies: planning and organizing (e.g., distancing), cognitive (e.g., consideration of future consequences), approaching others, regulating negative emotions (e.g., engaging in a pleasant activity), and external regulation (e.g., parents restricting usage time). Planning and organizing was the most frequently described strategy (70%), while external regulation was the least frequently described (7%). Older adolescents relied more frequently on DM and less on others to regulate negative emotions. Discussion The study's findings provide a contemporary map of self-regulatory strategies adolescents use to manage DM in their daily lives. The results underscore the centrality of planning and organizing strategies for adolescents' management of DM use. This heavier reliance on planning and organizing strategies may be a consequence of the highly rewarding and enticing characteristics of many DM platforms, which can curtail the efficacy of cognitive, in-the-moment strategies for inhibition. The relatively low reliance on parents for external regulation highlights adolescents' preference for regulating DM independently and underscores the importance of parental availability to support adolescents rather than regulating DM use for them. Finally, the increased reliance on DM to regulate negative emotions calls for further investigation into its long-term implications for emotion regulation.
