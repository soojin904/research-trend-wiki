## 초록

Pchum Ben is dedicated to honoring deceased ancestors and relatives whose spirits are believed to endure suffering due to negative karma from their lifetimes. They are understood to have become preta, or hungry ghosts. Observed annually from late September to early October, the festival blends Hindu, Theravāda Buddhist, and Southeast Asian Animist traditions. Its Hindu roots are reflected in the association with King Yama, the judge of the underworld and deity of death, who opens the gates of hell at this time of year. The preta are then allowed to return to the human realm, in search of food and merit. A central ritual, bay ben, involves tossing sticky rice balls toward the village edge and nearby wilderness. This belief motivates devotees to gather at Buddhist monasteries as early as 4:00 AM to toss bay ben (sticky rice balls) for these spirits, who are thought to be pacified by their consumption. The earliest reference to this practice appears in the K.279c inscription from King Yasovarman I's reign at Yasodharapura (now Angkor). Historical records indicate that similar rituals originally commemorated fallen soldiers rather than family ancestors (Davis 2016, 161). Documentation on the festival's evolution from the Angkorian period (9th to 15th centuries) is limited, but it likely adopted a stronger Theravāda Buddhist character after the 12th century. Nineteenth-century Khmer chronicles credit King Ang Duong (r. 1848–1860) with formalizing Pchum Ben into a structure that closely resembles its contemporary form (see Thun 2024, 24). This version of the festival spans fifteen days and centers on offering food and merit to elderly parents and deceased ancestors. Although the belief in King Yama persists, Theravāda Buddhism now predominates in Cambodia, shaping how the festival is understood and practiced. There are now offerings to monks, which have become a key aspect of the holiday, particularly as merit transfer is understood to benefit both the living and the deceased positively. Judy Ledgerwood argues that, following the collapse of the Khmer Rouge regime (April 1975–January 1979), the Pchum Ben festival has increasingly functioned as a site of community cohesion. As with many rituals, social networks are enacted, recreated, and displayed; through participation, individuals affirm their membership in various social groups (Ledgerwood 2012). This observation aligns closely with Erik Davis's interpretation of the festival as an exchange within a ritualized gift economy involving the spirits of the dead among contemporary Cambodians (2017, 160–188). It is also important to note that Pchum Ben is a national festival closely associated with material culture, including food, music, and gift-giving. As part of ritual practices and family reunions, families prepare traditional cakes such as num ansom chrouk (pork rice cake) and num kom (steamed rice cake wrapped in banana leaves). These cakes serve not only culinary but also symbolic religious functions, particularly in relation to the linga (in the case of num ansom) and yoni (in the case of num kom) within Hindu traditions. Similar to the Khmer New Year, many Cambodians travel to their home villages to visit elderly parents and offer them money, new clothes, and food. Visiting Buddhist monasteries together as a family remains a common practice, especially in rural areas. Finally, given its fifteen-day duration, Pchum Ben is also marked by a rich repertoire of songs that describe memorable aspects of the festival, including themes of returning home and honoring parents, as well as popular dance music such as ramvong. In addition, many monasteries broadcast the haunting song Daṃnuoñ Pret (Wailing of the Hungry Ghost) to remind people of the importance of participating in the festival.
