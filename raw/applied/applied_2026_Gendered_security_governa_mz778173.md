# Gendered security governance in Nigerian higher education institutions: Gender diversity, occupational segregation, and security effectiveness in Bauchi State

## 초록

Security governance in higher education has become a multidimensional institutional responsibility encompassing the protection of persons, infrastructure, information, institutional reputation, digital systems, and the continuity of academic and administrative functions. Yet security organizations in many Nigerian higher education institutions continue to operate within occupational cultures that privilege masculinized assumptions about authority, physical strength, surveillance, coercion, and command. This study rethinks gender roles in corporate security governance in six selected higher education institutions in Bauchi State, Nigeria, examining how formal rules, informal organizational norms, occupational expectations, and gender stereotypes shape access to strategic security responsibilities and how gender diversity relates to security effectiveness. The study adopted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design. Quantitative data were obtained from 196 security personnel and members of statutory security committees through stratified sampling, while qualitative evidence was generated through 24 purposively selected key-informant interviews and documentary analysis of institutional security policies and procedures. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, chi-square analysis, and hierarchical multiple regression; qualitative evidence was subjected to reflexive thematic analysis and integrated with quantitative results through joint displays. Findings demonstrate substantial gender differentiation across security functions. Women constituted 28.6% of respondents but only 12.2% of personnel occupying command positions. Women were disproportionately concentrated in gatekeeping, welfare, administrative monitoring, and student-facing functions, whereas men predominated in intelligence, investigation, physical response, and crisis command. The association between gender and security-role assignment was statistically significant, χ²(6, N = 196) = 42.31, p < .001. Hierarchical regression further indicated that gender diversity was positively associated with perceived security effectiveness (β = .34, p < .001), after accounting for resources and training. Institutions with more than 25% women reported higher levels of gender-based-violence reporting and faster responses to student complaints. Qualitative findings indicate that female security personnel function as important information brokers, legitimacy intermediaries, and access points for students who may be reluctant to approach male-dominated security structures. Drawing on Role Congruity Theory and Feminist Institutionalism, the study develops the GEMS Framework—Governance Restructuring, Empowerment Architecture, Metrics and Accountability, and Sensitivity/Cultural Redesign—as a gender-transformative model for higher education security governance. The article argues that gender inclusion should be understood not merely as an equality objective but as an institutional risk-management and security-capability strategy.
