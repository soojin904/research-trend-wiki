# Health behavior, loneliness and social participation among students in a higher education institution: a community case study providing insights into health psychology

**저자**: Sonia Lippke; Zhi Ye; Klaus Boehnke; Alexander Strelnikov; Lingling Gao
**출처**: Frontiers in Psychology, Vol.17
**발행일**: 2026-10-12
**DOI**: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1894036
**수집 키워드**: social network

## 초록

Background Higher education institutions can be conceptualized as community settings that may facilitate individuals’ health and well-being (in terms of low loneliness), health behavior, and general social participation. However, facilitating factors in individuals, their social network, and contextual factors were not researched building on the Evolutionary Theory of Loneliness and the Compensatory Carry-Over Action Model . Objective In a community case study, students’ health behavior, loneliness, and social participation were investigated to determine interrelations and factors that help students maintain a healthy lifestyle, reduce loneliness, and increase social participation. Method We analyzed an online survey of 373 students using descriptive statistics, group comparisons, regression models, and exploratory curve estimation. Results Of 293 students with available loneliness data, the mean loneliness was M = 2.76 (SD = 1.06; range 1–4), and 122 (41.6%) were classified as lonely. Loneliness was reflected in private relationships and study-related contacts, whereas no significant association was found with studying/working from home, χ 2 (1) = 0.11; p = 0.74. Lonely students differed from not-lonely students across 10 behavioral domains, including talking to a doctor, watching films, spending time in nature, interacting with others online, listening to music, drinking alcohol, eating, and shopping. For a healthy lifestyle, the regression results highlighted psychological resources and perceived health: self-efficacy predicted the healthy lifestyle indicator in Step 1 (OR = 2.40), and health literacy responsibility was significant in Step 2 (OR = 1.78), but poor subjective health was the strongest predictor in the final model (OR = 0.52; R 2 = 0.21). Loneliness was negatively associated with social participation (ß = −0.30), but this association weakened when further psychological and contextual factors were considered. Exploratory curve estimation indicated an inverted U-shaped association between stress and social participation among not-lonely students ( R 2 = 0.09; p = 0.03), whereas this pattern was not statistically significant among lonely students. Conclusion Students’ loneliness should not be understood as social disconnection, but as part of a broader health-psychological pattern involving health and well-being, psychological resources, contextual factors, and social participation within the higher education community.
