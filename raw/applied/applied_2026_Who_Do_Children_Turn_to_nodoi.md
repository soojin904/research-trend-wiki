# Who Do Children Turn to for Help Regulating Their Emotions? Intrinsic Interpersonal Emotion Regulation in Late Childhood

## 초록

Children often regulate their emotions with the help of multiple people within their social networks, including parents, peers, and teachers. In late childhood, children are increasingly expected to take an active role in managing their emotions, making it important to understand how they seek interpersonal emotion regulation support. The present study examined who children turn to for emotion regulation support and whether the way this support is organized is linked to their emotion regulation strategies and emotional functioning. Participants were 180 children aged 9–12 years (M = 10.72, SD = 1.06; 57% female; 75% White) who completed an interview in which they nominated specific people they would turn to for help regulating sadness, nervousness, and excitement and reported on characteristics of these relationships. Children also reported their use of cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression, and generalized anxiety symptoms. Parents reported on children’s self-management skills. Children’s emotion regulation support was organized primarily around family members and peers and embedded in close, long-standing relationships. Support across a wider range of emotions and reliance on different helpers for different emotions were associated with greater cognitive reappraisal, even after accounting for the number of helpers and relationship closeness. Greater relationship-type diversity was associated with lower parent-rated self-management. The organization of children’s emotion regulation support was unrelated to expressive suppression and generalized anxiety symptoms. These findings indicate that late childhood is a period in which interpersonal emotion regulation is actively organized across relationships, differentiated by emotional goals, and associated with children’s concurrent emotional functioning.
