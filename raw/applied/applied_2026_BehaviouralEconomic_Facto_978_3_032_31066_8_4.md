## 초록

Abstract Livestock disease epidemics have major economic, environmental, and public health consequences, making farmers’ adoption of prevention and control measures essential. This chapter reviews the behavioural-economic factors influencing farmers’ adoption of livestock disease prevention and control measures and examines how these insights can improve intervention design and epidemiological modelling. Drawing on behavioural frameworks developed in the broader agricultural economics literature, we review the animal health economics and related literature according to four groups of behavioural factors: (i) risk and time preferences, (ii) risk perceptions, heuristics, and cognitive biases, (iii) culture, social capital, and personality, and (iv) motivations and perceived abilities. We then link these factors to intervention functions and assess how they have been incorporated into bio-economic and epidemiological models. The review shows that effective interventions require distinguishing between relatively stable behavioural characteristics and more dynamic factors that can be influenced during disease events. Farmers respond not only to objective disease risks but also to emotions, social networks, and behavioural biases. Important research gaps remain, particularly regarding empirical evidence on risk and time preferences and the use of experimental and longitudinal research designs. Current epidemiological models largely represent decision-making as rational cost–benefit analysis and underrepresent behavioural complexity. Integrating richer behavioural-economic insights into epidemiological and bio-economic models can improve intervention design, strengthen voluntary disease prevention strategies, and support more effective livestock disease policy.
