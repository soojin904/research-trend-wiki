# Parenting at a Distance via Digital Communication: A Typology From Indonesian Migrant Worker Families

## 초록

ABSTRACT Children in migrant‐worker families increasingly rely on mobile phones and messaging apps to stay connected with parents, yet research remains unclear about whether more frequent contact improves children's emotional well‐being. This article examines Indonesian migrant‐worker families to explain how parenting at a distance via digital communication is practised and how recurring communication patterns shape children's emotional closeness and well‐being. Using multi‐actor qualitative evidence and giving interpretive priority to children's accounts where available, the study draws on interviews, focus group discussions, and complementary documentary materials from origin communities in East and West Java, alongside evidence on destination‐related constraints, including Hong Kong and Taiwan. We analyse contact through five communication features: mode, timing and predictability, content and emotional tone, responsiveness and repair, and facilitation of access and privacy. Cross‐case analysis of patterned combinations across these features identified four recurring patterns: Scheduled care, Asynchronous empathy, Control‐heavy contact and Caregiver‐facilitated contact. Children's closeness and well‐being appeared to depend less on contact frequency than on how contact was organised, facilitated and repaired. Experiences also varied with caregiving arrangements, device access and privacy, institutional routines and destination‐side work constraints. Scheduled care and Asynchronous empathy were more often linked to reassurance, felt availability and emotional attunement, whereas Control‐heavy contact and more restrictive facilitation were more often associated with strain and reduced openness. These findings suggest that children's emotional closeness and well‐being may be better understood through recurring communication patterns than through contact frequency alone, while pointing to more cautious, context‐sensitive research and practice.
