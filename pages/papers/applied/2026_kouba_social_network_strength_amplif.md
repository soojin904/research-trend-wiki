# Social network strength amplifies benefits of undergraduate ecology field programs

**저자**: Paige V. Kouba; Lina Arcila Hernández; Nikolas Kaplanis; L Balachandran; Haider Ali Bhatti; Roxanne S. Beltran; Erika Zavaleta
**출처**: , Vol.None
**발행일**: 2026-09-29
**DOI**: https://doi.org/10.22541/authorea.15009530/v1

## 초록 (원문)

A growing body of higher-education research supports the effectiveness of outdoor, field-based learning in the natural sciences. However, while the skills-based, cognitive, and affective benefits of field programs are increasingly well understood, the mechanisms behind those benefits require further investigation. We tested the hypothesis that social connections are linked to the affective benefits of field-based learning. We used pre- and post-program surveys and social network analysis to examine the relationship between social network development and self-assessed science identity gains among students in field-based learning experiences. We sought to understand (1) Whether social network strength predicts high science identity rating, before and/or after field-based learning; (2) How duration and structure of field-based learning programs influence social network development; and (3) Whether science identity ratings differed across demographic groups. Our study population was undergraduate students (N = 161) enrolled in six field-based learning programs at a large, R1 minority-serving institution. We found that after program participation, but not before, social network strength predicted high science identity ratings. Hence, while field-based learning programs strengthened science identity in general, they did so especially for students with strong social networks, whether that strength was maintained or added through program participation. Programs with longer duration and/or more field-based hours showed the greatest gains in student social network strength. Finally, students who identified with historically underrepresented race/ethnicity or gender groups, and first-generation college students, reported the largest gains in science identity, either ending their field-based learning programs with higher ratings than their well-represented peers, or (for first-generation students) closing a gap in ratings that existed before the programs. Our results illustrate that by strengthening existing social connections, and creating new ones, field-based programs lead to positive outcomes for student science identity. We recommend designing field-based experiences for community-building to enhance students’ personal and professional development.
